Suitability methodology

How a pile of maps becomes a class, a score and a reason for every plot. Version 0.1, matching src/land_intel/scoring.py.

1. Score each factor (fuzzy membership)

Each crop requirement defines nested bands: the S1 optimum inside the S2 band, inside the S3 band. Outside S3 the land is N. A factor value becomes a score between 0 and 1 that falls linearly as the value moves out of the optimum:

Where the value fallsFactor classScore
Inside the S1 bandS11.00
Between the S1 and S2 edgesS21.00 falling to 0.66
Between the S2 and S3 edgesS30.66 falling to 0.33
Beyond the S3 edgeN0

Range factors (altitude, temperature, rainfall, pH) can fail on both sides; the score is the worse of the two sides. Missing data never counts as suitable: it is reported as missing.

Worked example. Bread wheat, soil pH 5.3. The S2 band starts at 5.5 and the S3 band at 5.0, so pH 5.3 is in S3, 40% of the way from the S2 edge to the S3 edge: score = 0.66 − 0.33 × 0.4 = 0.53.

2. Weight the factors (AHP)

The Analytic Hierarchy Process turns expert judgement into weights. Instead of asking "what weight is rainfall?", which people answer inconsistently, it asks simple pairwise questions: "for teff, how much more important is rainfall than slope?" on Saaty's 1 to 9 scale. The weights are the principal eigenvector of the comparison matrix.

The consistency ratio (CR) checks the judgements against each other. Analogy: if rainfall beats slope 3 to 1 and slope beats pH 2 to 1, then rainfall should beat pH about 6 to 1. CR below 0.10 is accepted; above that, the panel revisits its judgements.

3. Combine the factors

A. FAO limiting factor

Class = the worst factor class. Score = the lowest factor score. Weakest-link logic, strict and transparent.

B. AHP weighted overlay with cap

Score = Σ weight × factor score. Class from the score: ≥ 0.80 S1, ≥ 0.60 S2, ≥ 0.40 S3, otherwise N. Then two safeguards: a weak factor caps the class one step above it, and any N factor makes the land N.

Why the cap? Without it, eight factors averaged together hide a single serious problem: in testing, plain averaging rated almost all non-N land as S1. The cap keeps the benefit of weighting while respecting the agronomic reality that one severe limitation cannot be fully offset.

4. Uncertainty

Each observation carries a standard deviation (for example, SoilGrids prediction intervals or the spread of field samples). The engine draws hundreds of plausible input sets (Monte Carlo), reruns the assessment and reports the spread of the score. A plot scored 0.82 ± 0.03 is a confident S1; 0.82 ± 0.15 is a candidate for a field visit.

The deep-learning model reports confidence through Monte Carlo dropout: it runs the network many times with random neurons switched off and measures how much the answers agree.

5. From cells to plots and clusters

Scores are computed on a 30 m grid, then summarised per plot polygon (area-weighted mean score, majority class, share of area in each class) and per planning cluster. Constraint masks (forest, water bodies, built-up land, protected areas) are applied before aggregation.

Known limitations of v0.1

  • Crop requirement thresholds are indicative and not yet calibrated with local trials.
  • Only Meher profiles for teff, bread wheat and maize exist so far.
  • Socio-economic suitability (market access, input access) is handled downstream in stages 04 and 11, not here.
  • The deep model has only been tested on synthetic data.
