The story of one season

How it works, step by step

Follow a farmers' cooperative through one year. The plan starts at the market in January, travels back to the land and the water, then runs forward through planting, harvest, storage, transport and fair payment, and ends with lessons that shape next year's plan.

The cooperative, its numbers and its buyer are illustrative. Each step links to the stage page with the method and an interactive demo.

  1. January · Stage 05

    Start with the buyer

    The cooperative and a flour mill agree what the mill needs after the next harvest: 900 tonnes of grade 2 wheat, delivered in April. Prices are forecast seven months ahead, with honest uncertainty.

    Output: a demand signal (minimum 900 t, maximum 1,400 t, planning price) and a price-band contract both sides will still want to honour at harvest.
    Open stage 05: Market intelligence →
  2. January · Stage 01

    Which land suits which crop?

    Soil, slope, altitude, rainfall and temperature are scored for every plot. Acidic plots are flagged as fixable with lime; steep plots stay out of the cereal plan.

    Output: suitability class (S1 to N), score, limiting factors and uncertainty for every plot, crop and season.
    Open stage 01: Land →
  3. February · Stage 02

    How much water, month by month?

    River flow (keeping water for the environment) and a share of groundwater recharge are compared with each crop's water need, month by month, because the lean month decides the irrigable area.

    Output: twelve monthly water limits per cluster and the maximum irrigable hectares per crop.
    Open stage 02: Water →
  4. March · Stage 03

    When will the rains come?

    Thirty years of daily weather become odds: the Kiremt rains have started by mid June in 8 years out of 10, and a 150-day maize fits the season only 2 years in 3.

    Output: a planting window, the chance each variety's cycle fits, and dry-spell, frost and drought risks.
    Open stage 03: Climate →
  5. March · Stage 04

    Make the plan

    The planner combines demand, suitability, monthly water and climate risk into one plan per cluster, and shows which limits bind: here, one more cubic metre of March water would be worth about 50 birr.

    Output: the seasonal production plan (cluster, crop, hectares, expected tonnes) and shadow prices that point to good investments.
    Open stage 04: Planning →
  6. March to May · Stage 06

    Order inputs backwards from planting day

    Seed, fertilizer and lime are ordered early enough to arrive on time in 9 cases out of 10: about 53 days ahead from the union, 17 from a local dealer. Shortfalls are flagged in March, not in June.

    Output: dated purchase orders with on-time probabilities, and early warnings.
    Open stage 06: Inputs →
  7. June to August · Stage 07

    Plant, tend and keep the timetable

    Willing households receive the crop each plot suits best. Every plot has an operations calendar; development agents record what was done, and visit first where most is at stake.

    Output: plot assignments, on-time rates by kebele and a weekly, ranked visit list.
    Open stage 07: Field →
  8. July to October · Stage 08

    Watch from space

    Every five days, each plot's greenness is compared with a healthy crop's growth curve. A pest outbreak on six plots triggers warnings by day 70, and the cluster production forecast tightens week by week.

    Output: alerts with likely causes, and yield forecasts with intervals for buyers, stores and trucks.
    Open stage 08: Monitoring →
  9. October to November · Stage 09

    Harvest in the right order

    Threshers go first to the plots whose window closes soonest. Grain is dried, graded and packed into lots, each one listing the plots it came from.

    Output: traceable lots (weight, moisture, grade, plots) and an account of every kilogram lost.
    Open stage 09: Harvest →
  10. November onwards · Stage 10

    Aggregate, store, decide when to sell

    Lots go to the nearest cooperative store. With hermetic bags, holding grain until May earns about 7% more than selling at the harvest low; with traditional bags it never pays.

    Output: collection centres, warehouse receipts for each farmer group, and store-or-sell advice.
    Open stage 10: Storage →
  11. November to December · Stage 11

    Move it with full trucks

    Pickups from twelve kebeles are combined into fewer, fuller trips, about 14% cheaper. Kebeles cut off by rainy-season road closures are cleared before the rains.

    Output: routes, shipments carrying traceable lots, and cost per tonne by kebele and season.
    Open stage 11: Logistics →
  12. December · Stage 12

    Pay fairly, measure honestly

    Each delivery is settled with transparent premiums and deductions, and every household receives its share through lot traceability. Incomes are compared with similar kebeles outside the pilot, so inflation is not mistaken for impact.

    Output: household payments, payment timeliness and the measured income effect.
    Open stage 12: Distribution →
  13. January again · The loop

    Learn, then start again

    Realised prices update the market model, realised yields per suitability class recalibrate the land model, and realised margins refine the planner. Next January starts from what really happened.

    Output: learning signals to stages 01, 04 and 05: the chain becomes a loop that improves every season.
